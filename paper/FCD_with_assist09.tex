% ABSTRACT
% ==============================================================================
\begin{abstract}
Cognitive Diagnosis Models (CDMs) are pivotal in Intelligent Tutoring Systems for evaluating students' latent knowledge states from historical learning interactions. 
However, traditional CDMs predominantly focus on a singular dimension of cognitive mastery, inherently neglecting the complex behavioral dynamics that accompany the learning process. 
By conflating a lack of problem-solving procedural fluency or incidental slips with a profound lack of fundamental knowledge, existing models often produce biased, coarse-grained, and pedagogically misleading diagnostic reports. 
To address this critical issue, we propose \mymethod\, a novel Multi-Task Learning (MTL) framework that explicitly decouples students' cognitive profiles into independent \textit{Mastery} and \textit{Fluency} dimensions. 
Specifically, our model introduces a secondary behavioral module driven by key proxy features, including learning opportunity, accuracy prior, and response stability. 
Furthermore, to prevent static feature entanglement, we design an intelligent dynamic fusion gate mechanism that self-adaptively calibrates the impact of cognitive mastery and behavioral fluency based on distinct exercise traits. 
Extensive experiments conducted on six real-world benchmark datasets demonstrate the universal superiority of \mymethod\ in prediction accuracy. 
Furthermore, in-depth interpretability analyses reveal that our framework provides granular, actionable, and multidimensional insights into student learning behaviors, effectively mitigating the risk of redundant pedagogical interventions.

\keywords{Cognitive Diagnosis \and Multi-Task Learning \and Behavioral Dynamics \and Educational Data Mining \and Interpretability.}
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Cognitive diagnosis is a fundamental task in educational data mining that aims to evaluate students' latent cognitive states based on their historical learning interactions and assessment records, as shown in Figure \ref{def:cd}. 
As a core driving mechanism of intelligent tutoring systems and massive open online courses, Cognitive Diagnosis Models (CDMs) provide essential quantitative support for personalized learning recommendations, adaptive testing, and intelligent curriculum design \cite{thai2010recommender}. 

Cognitive diagnosis research has evolved from traditional psychometric models to machine learning-based approaches, and more recently to paradigms based on deep learning. 
Psychometric-based models \cite{fan1998item,linden2000computerized,reckase2009multidimensional,delatorre2009dina} provide strong theoretical interpretability through explicit statistical modeling of cognitive states, but fail to capture complex learning dynamics due to rigid parametric assumptions. 
Learning latent cognitive states from historical interaction logs in a data-driven manner, machine learning-based models \cite{MF,SVM,FuzzyCDF} typically rely on manually designed features and shallow architectures, whereas deep learning-based models \cite{wang2020neural,wang2022neuralcd,zhang2025cognitive} also leverage neural networks to automatically capture complex, non-linear dependencies.

Despite continued algorithmic advances, most existing CDMs face notable limitations in practice. Their diagnoses rely almost exclusively on binary correctness logs, reducing cognitive states to a singular dimension of cognitive state, commonly referred to as knowledge mastery, while overlooking the rich behavioral dynamics inherent in the learning process. However, pedagogy and cognitive psychology \cite{smith1994misconceptions} distinguish declarative knowledge (i.e., knowing a concept) from procedural knowledge (i.e., fluently solving a problem) as qualitatively distinct cognitive dimensions. Therefore, conflating a lack of fluency with a lack of mastery inevitably leads to biased diagnostic outcomes. For example, a student may make a careless slip despite understanding fraction addition rules, and CDMs could not only misdiagnose this as non-mastery, but even trigger unnecessary interventions.

To address these issues, we propose \textbf{\mymethod}, a general framework designed to extend existing methods by naturally incorporating behavioral fluency into cognitive diagnosis to improve their performance. Our main contributions are summarized as follows:
\begin{itemize}
    \item We propose \mymethod\, a novel MTL-based cognitive diagnosis framework that explicitly decouples students' cognitive profiles into interpretable \textit{Mastery} and \textit{Fluency} dimensions, successfully bridging the gap between deep representation learning and educational psychology.
    \item {\color{blue}Our proposed \mymethod\ has high extendibility with existing CD methods, which easily improves previous CD studies that only model student-exercise interaction in a natural way.}
    \item We structurally formalize three mathematical proxy features (Opportunity, Accuracy Prior, Stability) and design an intelligent dynamic fusion gate mechanism. This plug-and-play architectural extension prevents deep networks from overfitting to non-cognitive anomalous slips.
    \item Extensive evaluations on six benchmark datasets with various CDM baselines clearly demonstrate the rationale and effectiveness of our \mymethod.
\end{itemize}

\section{Related Work}

\subsection{Psychometrics-based CDMs}
Traditional psychometric-based CDMs can be categorized into ability-level and cognition-level paradigms. The ability-level paradigm focuses on macro-level holistic evaluation, modeling a student's overall ability as a continuous latent trait to predict response probability, without explicitly diagnosing specific knowledge components. Examples include Item Response Theory (IRT) \cite{fan1998item} and its multidimensional extension (MIRT) \cite{reckase2009multidimensional}, which characterize exercises via parameters such as discrimination, difficulty and guessing. In contrast, the cognition-level paradigm adopts a micro-level diagnostic perspective, modeling a student's knowledge state as a fine-grained binary attribute vector (i.e., mastered or not mastered) over specific knowledge concepts, as defined by the Q-matrix. For example, the Deterministic Input, Noisy "And" gate (DINA) model ~\cite{delatorre2009dina,delatorre2011generalized} assumes that a correct response requires mastery of all concepts associated with the exercise.

Despite their theoretical interpretability, these traditional CDMs are often too rigid to capture complex and non-linear learning trajectories, and their parametric assumptions further constrain adaptability to high-dimensional interaction data.  

% The ability level paradigm is exemplified by Item Response Theory (IRT)\cite{fan1998item} and its multidimensional extension (MIRT)\cite{reckase2009multidimensional}, focuses on macro-level holistic evaluation. It aims to answer the question of "how strong is this student's overall ability" by modeling the probability of a correct response through a continuous latent ability variable. Parameters such as discrimination, difficulty, and guessing capture item characteristics, yet the output remains a global proficiency score without delving into specific knowledge structures. In contrast, the cognition level paradigm adopts a micro-level diagnostic perspective, addressing the more fundamental question: "Which specific knowledge points or skills has this student mastered, and where do the gaps remain?" The DINA model exemplifies this approach by representing a student's knowledge state as a binary attribute mastery vector, and explicitly employs a Q-matrix to specify the exact combination of skills required for each item. This fine-grained, attribute-level diagnosis provides actionable insights for targeted remediation, moving beyond the one-dimensional ability scores of traditional IRT frameworks.

\subsection{Learning-based CDMs}
In contrast to psychometrics-based models relying on strict theoretical assumptions, learning-based CDMs adopt a data-driven paradigm that directly learns latent cognitive states from historical interaction logs. These models are generally categorized into Machine Learning (ML)-based and Deep Learning (DL)-based approaches.

Early ML-based CDMs draw inspiration from collaborative filtering and matrix factorization, including matrix factorization~\cite{MF} and its probabilistic variant~\cite{PMF}, both of which factorize the student-exercise response matrix into low-dimensional latent trait vectors. Beyond matrix factorization, \cite{SVM} applies support vector machine to classify mastery states via maximum-margin hyperplanes. \cite{FuzzyCDF} employs a fuzzy set-theoretic framework that models continuous knowledge proficiency levels to accommodate both dichotomous and partially scored items. More recently, \cite{MvCRF} enriches cognitive profiling by extracting an effort trait from interaction logs, and integrating it with ability traits and a knowledge weakness parameter within a compensation mechanism.


DL-based CDMs leverage deep neural networks to automatically capture complex, non-linear interactions between students and exercises. 
Neural CDM (NCDM)~\cite{wang2020neural} introduces a neural framework with non-negative layers to enforce educational monotonicity, ensuring both accuracy and interpretability.
To address sparse knowledge coverage, Knowledge-association based extension of the Neural Cognitive Diagnosis model (KaNCD)~\cite{wang2022neuralcd} models concept associations via low-dimensional embeddings, while Knowledge Concept Cold-Start Cognitive Diagnosis model  (KCSCD)~\cite{zhang2026knowledge} targets concept cold-start via a gating-fused inference and ability module. 
Beyond concept-level modeling, the attention mechanism has also been explored in cognitive diagnosis. For example, Cognitive Diagnosis with Nonlinear Dependence between students and exercises (CDND)~\cite{zhang2025cognitive} employs an attention-like mechanism to capture nonlinear dependence between specific student-exercise pairs.

\section{Problem Formulation}

Let $\mathcal{U} = \{u_1, u_2, \dots, u_N\}$ denote the set of $N$ students, and $\mathcal{E} = \{e_1, e_2, \dots, e_M\}$ denote the set of $M$ exercises, $\mathcal{C} = \{c_1, c_2, \dots, c_K\}$ denote the set of $K$ knowledge concepts, and  $\mathcal{R}=\{(u_i,e_j,r_{ij})\mid u_i\in\mathcal{U}, e_j\in\mathcal{E}\}$ denote the set of response records, where binary response $r_{ij}$ equals 0 for an incorrect answer or 1 for a correct answer. 
The exercise-concept correlation matrix is encoded by a binary matrix named the Q-matrix, denoted as $\bm{Q}=[q_{jk}]_{M \times K}$. Specifically, $q_{jk}=1$ if concept $c_k$ is required to correctly answer item $e_j$, and $q_{jk}=0$ otherwise.

\begin{definition}\label{def:cd}
(Cognitive Diagnosis) Given the response records $\mathcal{R}$ and the Q-matrix $\bm{Q}$, cognitive diagnosis aims to estimate students' latent cognitive states from their interaction histories. These states are typically represented by knowledge proficiency $\alpha_{ik}$ of student $u_i \in \mathcal{U}$ on concept $c_k \in \mathcal{C}$, along with auxiliary item parameters such as difficulty and discrimination.
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}\label{def:dd}
Difficulty and discrimination parameters characterize exercise properties with respect to students' latent cognitive states. The difficulty parameter quantifies the baseline success probability for an average-proficiency student, while the discrimination parameter measures how sensitively the success probability responds to changes in cognitive states.
\end{definition}

For each exercise $e_j\in\mathcal{E}$, its difficulty and discrimination parameters are denoted as $h_j^{diff}$ and $h_j^{disc}$ ($j=1,2,\cdots,M$), respectively.

\section{Methodology}

The overall architecture of \mymethod\ is depicted in Figure \ref{fig:architecture}. The framework is systematically partitioned into three main, interconnected components: the Mastery Backbone (which captures traditional cognitive traits), the Behavioral Fluency Proxy (which encodes historical procedural states), and the Dynamic Fusion Gate (which adaptively bridges the two streams).

\begin{figure}[htbp]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{333.png}
\caption{The overall architecture of \mymethod\. The model processes inputs through a dual-stream design: the upper Mastery Backbone calculates fundamental cognitive competence via monotonicity-constrained layers, while the lower Behavioral Fluency Module extracts procedural familiarity. The two streams are adaptively merged via the Fusion Gate.}
\label{fig:architecture}
\end{figure}

\subsection{Mastery Backbone}
Following the foundational mathematical design of the standard NCDM \cite{wang2020neural}, we first map discrete student IDs $u$ and exercise IDs $e$ to continuous latent embeddings. 
These embeddings represent the student's conceptual cognitive proficiency ($h_u^S$) and specific exercise traits, namely exercise difficulty ($h_e^{diff}$) and exercise discrimination ($h_e^{disc}$). 

According to the core monotonicity assumption of cognitive diagnosis (i.e., a higher student proficiency should monotonically increase the probability of a correct response across all items), we construct the knowledge interaction matrix $x_{mastery}$. 
We utilize the exercise's predefined $Q$-matrix ($Q_e$) as a multi-hot mask to ensure that interactions strictly only occur on the concepts relevant to the specific question:
\begin{equation}
    x_{mastery} = h_e^{disc} \odot (h_u^S - h_e^{diff}) \odot Q_e
\end{equation}
where $\odot$ denotes the element-wise Hadamard product.

To rigorously preserve this psychological assumption of monotonicity throughout the deep neural network transformations, we utilize a constrained \textit{PosLinear} layer where all transformation weights are strictly penalized to remain non-negative during backpropagation:
\begin{equation}
    W_{pos} = 2 \cdot \text{ReLU}(-W) + W
\end{equation}
The base conceptual mastery prediction logit $o_{logit}$ is subsequently generated by passing the interaction matrix $x_{mastery}$ through a multi-layer perceptron (MLP) constructed exclusively with these \textit{PosLinear} layers.

\subsection{Behavioral Fluency Proxy Formulation}
To effectively capture the procedural learning dynamics that traditional models ignore, we formalize four behavioral features extracted from the raw interaction logs of student $u$ on exercise $e$ at time $T$:

\paragraph{Opportunity ($opp$).} The logarithmic cumulative encounter count with the target concept, preventing extreme values from dominating gradients:
\begin{equation}
    opp_{u, c}^{(T)} = \log \left( 1 + \sum_{t=1}^{T-1} \mathbb{I}(e_t \in \mathcal{C}_c) \right)
\end{equation}

\paragraph{Response Time ($rt$).} The raw response time in milliseconds. Longer response times typically indicate hesitation or lack of procedural fluency:
\begin{equation}
    \widetilde{rt} = -\log(1 + rt)
\end{equation}

\paragraph{Attempt Count ($att$).} The total number of attempts made on the exercise. Multiple attempts beyond the first suggest procedural difficulty:
\begin{equation}
    \widetilde{att} = -\log\bigl(1 + \max(0, attempt - 1)\bigr)
\end{equation}

\paragraph{Hint Count ($hint$).} The number of hints requested. Frequent hint usage indicates a lack of procedural fluency:
\begin{equation}
    \widetilde{hint} = -\log(1 + hint)
\end{equation}

These four behavioral signals are concatenated into a behavioral feature vector $B_u = [\widetilde{opp} \parallel \widetilde{rt} \parallel \widetilde{att} \parallel \widetilde{hint}]$. As visualized in Figure~\ref{fig:fluency_module}, a multi-layer behavioral encoder processes this vector to extract a high-dimensional latent fluency state $z \in \mathbb{R}^D$:
\begin{equation}
    z = \text{ReLU}(W_{beh} B_u + b_{beh})
\end{equation}

\begin{figure}[htbp]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.8\textwidth]{222.png}
\caption{The Behavioral Fluency Module. Four raw behavioral features (opportunity, response time, attempt count, hint count) are processed through logarithmic normalization and sign-based encoding, then passed through a multi-layer behavioral encoder to produce the latent fluency state $z$ and the scalar fluency score $flu$.}
\label{fig:fluency_module}
\end{figure}

To explicitly decouple the procedural state and compute a final, interpretable continuous fluency score $flu \in (0,1)$, we introduce a student-specific fluency bias embedding $b_{stu}$ and map the representation via a Sigmoid-activated projection head:
\begin{equation}
    flu = \sigma(\text{MLP}_{flu}(z + \tanh(b_{stu})))
\end{equation}

\subsection{Multi-Task Learning and Dynamic Fusion}
A significant mathematical challenge in dual-stream neural architectures is the risk of representation collapse, where the auxiliary behavioral encoder degenerates during end-to-end training if completely overshadowed by the primary binary cross-entropy loss. 
To rigorously mitigate this, we employ a Multi-Task Learning (MTL) regularization strategy. We introduce auxiliary regression heads that force the latent state $z$ to faithfully reconstruct the original proxy statistical features. The total loss function $\mathcal{L}_{total}$ is balanced using a hyper-parameter $\lambda$:
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{L}_{total} = \mathcal{L}_{y} + \lambda \cdot (\mathcal{L}_{opp} + \mathcal{L}_{acc} + \mathcal{L}_{sta})
\end{equation}
where $\mathcal{L}_{opp}, \mathcal{L}_{acc}, \mathcal{L}_{sta}$ are computed using Mean Squared Error (MSE) against the ground truth proxies.

Finally, to address the profound rigidity of static feature concatenation commonly seen in prior works, a dynamic fusion gate (illustrated in Figure \ref{fig:gate_mechanism}) computes an adaptive weighting scalar $g \in (0,1)$. This gate processes the non-linear interaction between the behavioral latent state $z$ and the specific traits of the target exercise:
\begin{equation}
    g = \sigma(\text{MLP}_{gate}([z \parallel h_e^{diff} \parallel h_e^{disc}]))
\end{equation}

The final prediction probability $\hat{y}$ is calibrated by the gate via a mathematically interpretable residual correction mechanism applied to the core mastery logit:
\begin{equation}
    core' = o_{logit} + \gamma \cdot g \cdot (flu - 0.5) \cdot 4.0
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
    \hat{y} = \sigma(core')
\end{equation}

\begin{figure}[htbp]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.8\textwidth]{111.png}
\caption{The Dynamic Fusion Gate mechanism. It explicitly evaluates item characteristics to dynamically combine behavioral inputs with the core conceptual logit, executing an adaptive residual adjustment that prevents overconfidence.}
\label{fig:gate_mechanism}
\end{figure}

In Equation 11, the term $(flu - 0.5)$ mathematically acts as a directional penalty or bonus. If a student is highly fluent ($flu > 0.5$), it provides a positive residual push to the core mastery logit, rewarding their procedural familiarity. Conversely, if procedural fluency is poor ($flu < 0.5$), it strictly penalizes the logit. Crucially, the item-aware gate activation $g$ determines the absolute \textit{magnitude} of this penalty, empowering the model to heavily rely on the fluency branch for routine procedural questions, while reverting strictly to the mastery branch for novel, complex conceptual problems.

\subsubsection{Evaluation Metrics}
All methods are evaluated via four commonly-used metrics in KT field, including Area Under the ROC Curve (AUC), Accuracy (ACC), F1 Score (F1), and Root Mean Square Error (RMSE).

% Finally, Root Mean Square Error (\textbf{RMSE}) is calculated against the continuous probability predictions to gauge the absolute error margin, evaluating the model's confidence calibration.

\subsubsection{Implementation Details}
\mymethod\ and all baseline models are implemented using the PyTorch deep learning library. All models are trained with the AdamW optimizer to prevent weight decay interference, with an initial learning rate dynamically selected from $\{0.001, 0.002, 0.005\}$ and a Cosine Annealing learning rate schedule for smooth convergence. A uniform dropout rate of $0.3$ is applied to all fully connected layers to strictly mitigate overfitting.
All models are trained on NVIDIA RTX 4090 GPUs with a batch size of 256. We adopt an early stopping strategy with a patience of 5 epochs to determine the optimal stopping point and extract the best generalized weights.

\subsection{Comparison Experiments}

Table~\ref{tab:comparison_exp1} reports the performance comparison on six benchmark datasets under the standard scenario. As can be observed, \mymethod\ consistently achieves superior or competitive performance against all baseline models across nearly all evaluation metrics and datasets. Notably, the performance gains are especially pronounced for traditional psychometrics-based models such as IRT, MIRT, and DINA, which lack inherent capacity for capturing complex behavioral dynamics. Even for state-of-the-art DL-based models such as KaNCD, CDND, and KCSCD, \mymethod\ consistently yields performance improvements across most evaluation metrics, demonstrating its broad applicability and architectural compatibility. 
Table~\ref{tab:comparison_exp2} presents results on ASSIST09 dataset under the enriched scenario. Under this condition, all baseline models exhibit substantially greater performance improvements, especially for the traditional psychometrics-based models. These results demonstrate that direct access to behavioral proxies enables our dual-stream architecture to sharply distinguish true conceptual deficits from procedural unfamiliarity or incidental slips.

Overall, across both standard and enriched scenarios, the comparison results consistently support the hypothesis that decoupling mastery from fluency provides a universally beneficial enhancement for cognitive diagnosis.

\subsection{Ablation Study}
To deeply and rigorously evaluate the specific architectural contribution of each internal component, we systematically deconstructed our framework into three hierarchical variants, mathematically defined as follows:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{L1 (Only Fluency):} Strips away both the MTL behavioral encoder and the dynamic fusion gate. It treats "fluency" merely as a static, learnable embedding vector $E_{flu}$ without proxy constraints: $\hat{y} = \sigma(\text{MLP}(x_{mastery}) + W_{flu} E_{flu})$.
    \item \textbf{L2 (Fluency + Behavior):} Re-introduces the explicit behavioral proxy features and MTL regularization, but lacks the dynamic fusion gate, forcing the network to rely on simple static concatenation: $\hat{y} = \sigma(\text{MLP}([x_{mastery} \parallel z]))$.
    \item \textbf{L3 (Full \mymethod):} The complete, state-of-the-art framework equipped with both the structured MTL encoder and the adaptive dynamic fusion gate as mathematically defined in Equations 10, 11, and 12.
\end{itemize}

As thoroughly detailed in Table \ref{tab:ablation}, the NCDM baseline (L0) yields a poor AUC of 0.6923, indicating that raw correctness prediction alone is insufficient for accurate diagnosis. Adding a simple fluency embedding (L1) provides only marginal improvement (0.6977). Incorporating explicit behavioral proxy features without the dynamic gate (L2) raises AUC to 0.7178. However, the definitive leap to 0.9626 is achieved only with the full framework (L3), demonstrating that the adaptive fusion gate is indispensable. 

Furthermore, Figure \ref{fig:ablation_bar} provides a clear visual comparison of the performance metrics across all six variants, highlighting the step-wise gains achieved. While capturing behavioral features is undeniably important, the \textit{method} of fusing them with cognitive mastery is the true architectural bottleneck. Static fusion treats all questions identically, leading to severe feature dilution; the dynamic fusion gate successfully learns an item-aware routing mechanism.

\subsection{Case Study and Interpretability Analysis (RQ3)}
A primary, overriding motivation for developing the \mymethod\ framework is to transition EDM from black-box correctness predictions to highly transparent, high-resolution cognitive profiling. To conclusively address \textbf{RQ3}, we conduct a qualitative, visual analysis focusing on the decoupling of cognitive states and the internal mathematical activation of the dynamic fusion gate.

\subsubsection{Decoupling Mastery and Fluency (Radar Profiles):}
Traditional CDMs structurally output a single scalar value per knowledge point. Figure \ref{fig:radar_case} illustrates a comparative diagnostic profile for two specific students (Student\#1 and Student\#2) across five distinct knowledge points (labeled A to E, representing domains such as Arithmetic, Fractions, Geometry, Algebra, and Probability). 

As clearly shown in Figure \ref{fig:radar_case}(a), the standard NCDM evaluates Student\#1 as strictly and globally superior to Student\#2 across all domains. From a practical pedagogical standpoint, this flat assessment would prompt an educator or an ITS algorithm to blindly assign extensive remedial conceptual lectures to Student\#2. 

However, when these identical interaction logs are evaluated through our decoupled \mymethod\ framework (Figure \ref{fig:radar_case}b), a highly nuanced, actionable reality emerges. The model successfully decouples the profile, revealing that Student\#1 and Student\#2 actually share highly comparable, fundamental \textit{Mastery} levels on core concepts (particularly domains A, B, and C). The severe performance disparity observed originally stems almost entirely from the \textit{Fluency} dimension (the procedural inability to execute the task quickly under testing conditions). In a real-world tutoring system, this dictates an entirely different, optimized intervention: Student\#2 requires targeted, time-constrained practice exercises to build problem-solving fluency, rather than enduring redundant theoretical retraining.

\caption{Comparative diagnostic profiles. (a) Standard NCDM yields a single, entangled mastery metric. (b) \mymethod\ successfully decouples the cognitive state, revealing nuanced differences in behavioral fluency despite similar conceptual mastery.}

\end{figure}

\subsubsection{Dynamic Gate Adaptation (Numerical Walkthrough \& Bar Charts):}
Figure \ref{fig:bar_case} vividly illustrates the model's internal routing mechanism for two distinct students across two distinct knowledge points (KP\#79 and KP\#82). The left panel explicitly demonstrates that the gate value is highly variable and entirely item-aware. The dynamic gate essentially functions as an intelligent cognitive attention mechanism. 

To provide a concrete numerical walkthrough: consider Student\#2827 on KP\#79 (a highly procedural task, e.g., "Simplifying Fractions"). The model extracts a latent fluency score $z_{flu} = 0.21$ (indicating poor procedural familiarity). Because the question is highly procedural, the dynamic gate $g$ activates at a high level of $0.85$. Following Equation 11, the residual adjustment applied to the logit is mathematically $\gamma \cdot 0.85 \cdot (0.21 - 0.5) \cdot 4.0 \approx -0.98$. This mathematically executes a sharp, severe penalty on the final prediction logit (as seen in the deep orange bar extending downwards in the right panel).

Conversely, for KP\#82 (a deep conceptual task, e.g., "Understanding Limits"), the gate activation drops significantly ($\approx 0.3$), dampening the $(flu-0.5)$ penalty and forcing the model to disregard procedural slips and rely heavily on the fundamental cognitive mastery branch. This definitively proves that \mymethod\ effectively learns a self-adaptive routing strategy.

\section{Conclusion}

In this paper, we proposed a novel and highly interpretable Multi-Task Learning framework that successfully bridges the historical gap between deep cognitive modeling and practical behavioral dynamics. To directly overcome the critical limitations of traditional unidimensional models, our framework explicitly decouples students' cognitive profiles into distinct \textit{Mastery} and \textit{Fluency} dimensions. By introducing key behavioral proxy features (Opportunity, Accuracy Prior, Stability) and engineering an intelligent dynamic fusion gate, our plug-and-play module universally and significantly enhances the predictive performance of all tested baseline models across diverse real-world educational datasets. {\color{blue}Extensive experiments on real-world datasets clearly showed the effectiveness and extendibility of our RCD framework.}
